# Principles

## P1 — Invariance is relational

Nothing is "an invariant" in a useful ITF sense without specifying what transformation it is invariant under.

## P2 — Boundaries are part of the claim

A statement that omits its validity boundary is weaker than one that exposes its assumptions.

## P3 — Learn the delta, not the branding

Prior knowledge should be reused when the mapping survives testing. Deep learning effort should concentrate on what remains irreducibly new.

## P4 — Similarity is not equivalence

Shared invariants do not make two systems identical.

## P5 — Abstraction is lossy

A useful abstraction hides irrelevant variation. A harmful abstraction hides relevant variation.

## P6 — Analogy must be attacked

A proposed mapping gains credibility by surviving attempts to break it.

## P7 — Operational knowledge still matters

Syntax, tooling, and implementation details are necessary when they affect execution, correctness, performance, safety, or delivery. ITF does not replace hands-on competence.

## P8 — Novelty must be located, not denied

The framework is successful when it distinguishes recomposition from genuine changes in mechanisms, constraints, or capabilities.

## P9 — Uncertainty should remain visible

Unknowns and low-confidence mappings belong in the analysis rather than being hidden behind confident terminology.

## P10 — Framework claims are revisable

ITF itself is subject to the same discipline: its concepts may be refined, rejected, or narrowed when counterexamples demand it.
